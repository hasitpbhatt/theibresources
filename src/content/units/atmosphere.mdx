## Structure and composition

Dry air is roughly 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, 0.93% argon, 0.04% carbon dioxide — with trace
gases and variable water vapour making up the rest. The gases that matter for climate are the
ones present in parts per million or less.

### Layers

| Layer | Extent | Key feature |
|---|---|---|
| Troposphere | 0–12 km | Weather, most mass, temperature falls with height |
| Stratosphere | 12–50 km | Ozone layer, temperature rises with height |
| Mesosphere | 50–85 km | Meteors burn up |
| Thermosphere | 85 km+ | Ionised, aurorae |

The temperature profile matters. A layer that warms with height is **stable** and resists
vertical mixing — which is why the stratosphere is stratified, and why pollutants that reach it
stay there for years.

### The lapse rate

In the troposphere, temperature falls about 6.5 °C per km. Warm air rises, expands, cools. When
the rising air reaches the dew point, clouds form. This is the engine of most weather.

An **inversion** occurs when a warm layer sits above cold air. Vertical mixing stops, and
pollutants accumulate beneath the lid. Most severe urban smog episodes are inversion events.

## Insolation and albedo

Solar energy arriving at the top of the atmosphere is roughly 1361 W/m². Averaged over the
sphere and adjusted for reflection, the surface receives about a quarter of that per unit area.

**Albedo** is the fraction reflected. Fresh snow around 0.85, desert sand around 0.40, forest
around 0.15, open ocean around 0.06.

### Why albedo feedbacks matter

Ice reflects; ocean absorbs. Lose ice, absorb more, warm further, lose more ice. This is a
**positive feedback**, and it is one of the reasons polar regions warm faster than the global
average.

### Differential heating

Tropical regions receive more energy per unit area than the poles. The atmosphere and oceans
move heat poleward to compensate. That transport is the reason mid-latitude weather exists.

## The greenhouse effect

Shortwave solar radiation passes through the atmosphere largely unimpeded and is absorbed by
the surface. The surface re-emits energy as longwave infrared. Greenhouse gases absorb specific
infrared wavelengths and re-radiate in all directions, including back down.

The mechanism is not "trapping heat" — it is delaying its escape. Each absorption and re-emission
adds a step before energy leaves the top of the atmosphere.

### The important nuance

The greenhouse effect is not a problem. Without it, Earth's mean surface temperature would be
around −18 °C. The problem is the **enhancement** of it: adding gases faster than the system can
adjust.

### Which gases, and why the ranking differs

| Gas | Relative strength per molecule | Atmospheric lifetime |
|---|---|---|
| CO₂ | 1 (reference) | Centuries to millennia |
| CH₄ | ~28 over 100 years | ~12 years |
| N₂O | ~265 over 100 years | ~120 years |
| CFCs | thousands | decades to centuries |

Methane is far stronger per molecule but short-lived; CO₂ is weaker but accumulates. This is
why the choice of time horizon changes the ranking, and why "which gas is worst" is a badly
posed question.

**Global warming potential (GWP)** is the comparison over a stated horizon, usually 100 years.
Always state the horizon.

## Stratospheric ozone

Ozone (O₃) in the stratosphere absorbs ultraviolet radiation. It is produced and destroyed
continuously:

```
O₂ + UV → 2O
O + O₂ → O₃
```

### Depletion chemistry

Chlorofluorocarbons are stable in the troposphere, so they survive to the stratosphere, where UV
breaks them apart and releases chlorine. A single chlorine atom can destroy thousands of ozone
molecules in a catalytic cycle:

```
Cl + O₃ → ClO + O₂
ClO + O → Cl + O₂
```

The chlorine is regenerated, so it acts as a catalyst. This is why small concentrations caused
large effects.

### The policy lesson

The Montreal Protocol worked because the chemistry was clear, substitutes existed, and the
industry could be moved. It is the standard example of a successful environmental treaty — and
the reason it worked is worth studying as carefully as the chemistry.

**Note the confusion risk**: stratospheric ozone depletion and tropospheric ozone pollution are
opposite problems. Ozone high up is protective; ozone at ground level is a respiratory
irritant and a component of photochemical smog.

## Air pollution and smog

### Photochemical smog

Nitrogen oxides and volatile organic compounds react in sunlight to produce ozone, aldehydes
and peroxyacetyl nitrate (PAN). Requires sunlight, so it peaks in summer afternoons.

Conditions that worsen it: strong sun, high temperature, low wind, inversion layer, heavy
traffic.

### Particulate matter

PM₁₀ (under 10 µm) and PM₂.₅ (under 2.5 µm). The smaller fraction penetrates deeper into the
lungs and is the more serious health concern. Sources include combustion, dust, and secondary
formation from sulphur and nitrogen oxides.

### Acid deposition

Sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides oxidise to sulphuric and nitric acid, then deposit wet (as
acid rain) or dry. Effects: acidification of lakes and soils, aluminium mobilisation, damage to
foliage and stone.

The intervention that worked was **flue gas desulphurisation** and low-sulphur fuel — attacking
the source rather than the symptom.

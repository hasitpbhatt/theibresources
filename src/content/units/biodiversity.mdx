## Measuring biodiversity

Biodiversity is measured at three levels, and mixing them up is the most common error.

- **Genetic diversity** within a population — the raw material for adaptation.
- **Species diversity** within a community — richness plus evenness.
- **Habitat/ecosystem diversity** across a landscape — the variety of places.

### Species richness and evenness

**Richness** is how many species are present. **Evenness** is how similar their abundances
are. A lawn with 20 species where one covers 95% of the area is not the same as a meadow with
20 species spread evenly, even though both have the same richness.

The **Shannon index** combines both:

```
H = − Σ (pᵢ × ln pᵢ)
```

Where `pᵢ` is the proportion of individuals in species `i`. Higher `H` means more diverse.
A community dominated by one species gives a low value even if richness is high — which is
exactly the behaviour you want from a diversity measure.

### Sampling

You almost never count everything. You sample, and the sampling design determines what you can
legitimately conclude.

- **Random sampling** supports estimates about the whole area.
- **Systematic (transect/quadrat grid)** reveals gradients and patterns.
- **Stratified sampling** handles visibly different zones.

State your method and your limits. A quadrat thrown where the flowers are is not random, and
the data will not support a claim about the field.

## Why diversity matters

Four distinct arguments, often conflated:

1. **Ecological resilience** — more species means more functional redundancy. If one fails,
   another may cover its role.
2. **Ecosystem services** — pollination, water filtration, pest control, soil formation.
3. **Direct use** — food, medicine, fibre, tourism.
4. **Intrinsic and cultural value** — existence, heritage, identity.

Arguments 1–3 are instrumental and measurable. Argument 4 is not, and pretending otherwise
weakens it. Say which one you are making.

## Threats and drivers

The five direct drivers, in rough order of global impact:

- **Habitat loss and fragmentation** — the largest single cause.
- **Overexploitation** — hunting, fishing, harvest beyond replacement.
- **Invasive species** — competition, predation, disease.
- **Pollution** — nutrients, plastics, pesticides, noise, light.
- **Climate change** — shifting ranges, phenological mismatch, bleaching, fire regimes.

### Why fragmentation is worse than area loss

Losing half a forest is bad. Losing half a forest in a way that leaves ten isolated fragments
is worse. Edge effects penetrate inward, small populations lose genetic diversity, and
dispersal between patches becomes a gamble. **Connectivity** is often a better conservation
target than raw area.

## Conservation approaches

### In situ

Protecting species where they live: reserves, national parks, marine protected areas,
community conservancies. Cheaper, ecologically richer, but exposed to whatever happens around
the boundary.

### Ex situ

Protecting species away from their habitat: botanic gardens, seed banks, captive breeding,
cryopreservation. A last resort, and an insurance policy rather than a solution — a species
that exists only in a seed bank has lost its ecological role.

### The design of reserves

- **Size**: bigger is generally better, but one large reserve and several medium ones can be
  comparable depending on species.
- **Shape**: compact beats ribbon-shaped, because edge-to-area ratio is lower.
- **Connectivity**: corridors allow gene flow and range shifts.
- **Buffers**: transitional zones reduce edge effects.

### Community-based conservation

Conservation that excludes the people living in the landscape tends to fail. Approaches that
give local communities a stake — managed resource rights, benefit sharing, co-management —
tend to last longer, though they are harder to design and much harder to measure.

## The honest summary

Conservation works when the incentives of the people who control the land align with keeping
it intact. Everything else — fences, laws, campaigns — is a way of buying time until that
alignment exists.

## Radiative forcing

**Radiative forcing** is a change in the energy balance of the Earth system, measured in
watts per square metre at the top of the atmosphere. Positive forcing warms; negative cools.

| Driver | Approximate forcing (W/m²) |
|---|---|
| CO₂ increase | +2.2 |
| Other well-mixed greenhouse gases | +1.0 |
| Halocarbons | +0.4 |
| Aerosols (direct and indirect) | −1.0 |
| Surface albedo change | −0.15 |
| Solar variation | +0.05 |

Two things to take from this table. Aerosols offset a meaningful fraction of greenhouse
warming — which means cleaning up air pollution unmasks warming that is already committed.
And solar variation is an order of magnitude too small to explain the observed trend.

## Feedbacks

### Positive

- **Ice–albedo** — melting ice exposes darker surfaces, absorbing more energy.
- **Water vapour** — warmer air holds more water vapour, which is itself a greenhouse gas. This
  is the single largest feedback and it is amplifying.
- **Permafrost carbon** — thawing releases CO₂ and methane.
- **Forest dieback** — warming and drought convert carbon sinks into sources.

### Negative

- **Increased plant growth** — CO₂ fertilisation and longer growing seasons.
- **Increased weathering** — silicate weathering consumes CO₂, but on geological timescales.
- **Longwave radiation** — warmer surface radiates more, following the Stefan–Boltzmann
  relationship. This scales as T⁴.

The negative feedbacks are real but slow or weak; the positive ones are fast. That asymmetry is
the whole problem.

## Evidence and attribution

### Lines of evidence

1. **Direct measurement** — surface and ocean temperature records, instrumental since the 1850s.
2. **Proxies** — ice cores (CO₂ and temperature over 800,000 years), tree rings, sediments,
   corals.
3. **Physical fingerprints** — stratospheric cooling with tropospheric warming is the signature
   of greenhouse forcing, not solar forcing. The sun would warm both.
4. **Isotopic composition** — fossil carbon has a distinct ¹³C/¹²C signature, and the
   atmosphere's ratio has shifted in the direction that fossil fuel combustion predicts.
5. **Ocean heat content** — over 90% of the excess energy goes into the ocean, which is why sea
   level and ocean heat are more reliable indicators than surface air temperature.

### Attribution

Attribution asks how much of an observed change is due to a specific cause. The method is
detection-and-attribution: run climate models with and without the forcing in question, and
compare the resulting distributions against observations.

The key result: natural forcings alone cannot reproduce the observed late-20th-century warming,
but natural plus anthropogenic forcings can.

## Impacts and vulnerability

### Physical

Sea level rise (thermal expansion plus ice loss), intensification of heavy rainfall, longer and
hotter heatwaves, shifts in precipitation belts, ocean acidification.

### Biological

Range shifts poleward and upslope, phenological mismatch (timing of events decoupling — birds
arriving after caterpillars have peaked), coral bleaching, pest and disease range expansion.

### Human

Crop yield changes, water stress, heat mortality, displacement, damage from extreme events.

### Vulnerability

Vulnerability is a function of **exposure**, **sensitivity**, and **adaptive capacity**. Two
places with identical physical exposure can have wildly different outcomes because one has
insurance, early warning and infrastructure and the other does not.

This is why climate change is discussed as an equity problem, not only a physics problem.

## Mitigation and adaptation

**Mitigation** reduces the cause — emissions. **Adaptation** reduces the harm — prepares for
the change already committed.

You need both, and they are not substitutes. Mitigation is a global public good (everyone
benefits, whoever pays); adaptation is largely local and private (you benefit from what you
build).

### Mitigation levers

- Decarbonise electricity.
- Electrify transport and heating.
- Reduce non-CO₂ emissions (methane leakage, agriculture, refrigerants).
- Protect and restore carbon sinks.
- Improve efficiency.
- Change consumption patterns.

### Adaptation levers

- Early warning systems.
- Flood defences and managed retreat.
- Drought-resistant crops and changed planting calendars.
- Heat action plans and urban greening.
- Insurance and social protection.

### Tipping points

Thresholds beyond which a system reorganises into a different state, usually irreversibly on
human timescales. Candidates include ice sheet collapse, Amazon dieback, permafrost carbon
release, and coral reef bleaching beyond recovery.

The uncertainty is not whether they exist but where the thresholds are and how they interact.
Cascading tipping — one triggering another — is the scenario that justifies urgency beyond
linear risk estimates.

### Carbon budgets

A carbon budget is the cumulative CO₂ that can be emitted for a given probability of staying
below a temperature threshold. Because CO₂ accumulates, the budget is a **total**, not a rate.
Spending it faster does not spend more of it — but it does raise the peak, and the peak is what
drives impacts.

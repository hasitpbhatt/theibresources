## Species and populations

A **species** is a group that interbreeds to produce fertile offspring. The definition has
awkward edges — asexual organisms, ring species, hybrids — but for field ecology the practical
version is: a group that consistently looks and behaves like one thing.

A **population** is the individuals of one species in a defined area at a defined time. Both
definitions are decisions. State them and your data become interpretable; leave them implicit
and two people can count the same meadow and disagree entirely.

### Population growth

Exponential growth assumes unlimited resources:

```
dN/dt = rN
```

Logistic growth adds a ceiling:

```
dN/dt = rN (K − N) / K
```

Where `r` is the intrinsic rate of increase and `K` is carrying capacity. The gap between the
two models is not mathematical trivia — it is the difference between a species arriving in an
empty habitat and the same species arriving in an occupied one.

### Limiting factors

- **Density-dependent**: competition, disease, predation. Impact grows as density grows.
- **Density-independent**: fire, drought, flood. Impact is unrelated to how crowded the
  population is.

Real populations feel both, and the interesting question is which one is currently binding.

## Communities and niches

A **community** is all the populations in an area. A **niche** is the sum of conditions and
resources a species uses — its address plus its occupation.

The **fundamental niche** is what a species could use with no competitors. The **realised
niche** is what it actually uses once competition is present. The gap between them is a
measure of how much the species is being pushed around.

### Competitive exclusion and coexistence

Two species cannot occupy the same niche indefinitely — one will outcompete the other. Yet
coexistence is everywhere, because niches differ in time, in space, or in resource type.
Resource partitioning is the usual answer to "how do all these species live here?"

## Energy flow through food webs

Roughly 10% of energy transfers between trophic levels. The other 90% is lost as heat through
respiration, or exits as uneaten parts, or is never captured in the first place.

This single number explains a lot:

- Why top predators are rare and need large territories.
- Why food chains are short — there is simply not enough energy left for a seventh level.
- Why eating lower on the chain feeds more people per hectare.

### Productivity

- **Gross primary productivity (GPP)**: total energy captured by producers.
- **Net primary productivity (NPP)**: GPP minus the producers' own respiration — the energy
  actually available to the next level.

NPP is what ecologists measure when they want to know how productive a system really is.
Deserts and open ocean are low; tropical forest, wetlands and estuaries are high.

## Nutrient cycles

### Carbon

Fast cycle: photosynthesis and respiration, measured in years. Slow cycle: fossil formation and
weathering, measured in millions of years. Human emissions take carbon out of the slow cycle
and inject it into the fast one. That mismatch of timescales is the whole problem.

### Nitrogen

Atmospheric nitrogen is inert; almost nothing can use it directly. Fixation — biological,
industrial, or by lightning — is the bottleneck. Human industrial fixation now rivals all
natural fixation combined, which is why nitrogen pollution is a global issue rather than a
local one.

### Phosphorus

No gaseous phase, so the cycle is sedimentary and slow. Phosphorus is usually the limiting
nutrient in freshwater. That is why phosphate detergent and fertiliser runoff cause
eutrophication so reliably.

## Succession and climax

**Primary succession** starts on bare substrate with no soil — lava flows, retreating glaciers,
new sand dunes. **Secondary succession** starts where soil survives — after fire, clearing, or
abandonment.

The sequence is broadly predictable in its *stages*, not in its *species*: pioneer species,
then rapid colonisers, then competitive dominators, with biodiversity typically peaking in
mid-succession and net productivity levelling off late.

The old idea of a single stable **climax community** is now treated cautiously. Disturbance is
frequent enough that many systems are better described as a shifting mosaic than as a
destination.

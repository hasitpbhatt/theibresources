## Why markets underprice nature

Neoclassical economics handles environmental problems badly for three specific reasons, and
naming them precisely is more useful than general complaint.

### Externalities

An **externality** is a cost or benefit borne by someone who did not choose it. Pollution is the
textbook negative externality: the producer pays for inputs but not for the atmosphere's
assimilation capacity.

The consequence is systematic overproduction of the externality, because the price signal is
incomplete. Nothing ideological — simply a missing term in the cost function.

### Common pool resources

Rivalrous but non-excludable: fish stocks, groundwater, clean air. Each user's rational
individual action degrades the resource for all. Hardin's "tragedy of the commons" is the
folk version; Ostrom's work on self-governance is the empirically accurate one, showing that
communities do solve these problems when boundaries, rules and monitoring are locally legible.

### Public goods

Non-rivalrous and non-excludable: climate stability, biodiversity, scientific knowledge. Markets
undersupply these because the provider cannot capture the benefit. The standard responses are
public provision, subsidy, or some form of collective financing.

## Valuing the environment

### Use values

- **Direct use** — timber, fish, water, recreation.
- **Indirect use** — ecosystem services: flood attenuation, pollination, water filtration.
- **Option value** — the value of keeping the option open for future use.

### Non-use values

- **Existence value** — value from knowing something exists.
- **Bequest value** — value from passing it to future generations.

### Valuation methods

| Method | How it works | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Market price | Observed price of traded goods | Only covers traded goods |
| Revealed preference (travel cost, hedonic) | Infer value from behaviour | Confounded by other factors |
| Stated preference (contingent valuation) | Ask willingness to pay | Hypothetical bias, scope insensitivity |
| Replacement cost | Cost to substitute the service | Assumes substitution is possible |
| Avoided damage cost | Cost of damage prevented | Requires damage function |

**Willingness to pay** and **willingness to accept** diverge sharply, and the divergence is
larger for environmental goods than for ordinary ones. This is not irrationality — it reflects
that loss and gain are not symmetric.

### The discount rate problem

Cost-benefit analysis discounts future benefits. A high discount rate makes distant harms nearly
worthless; a low one makes almost any long-term project look justified.

The choice of discount rate often determines the conclusion more than any other input. Say what
rate you used and why. If you cannot, you have not done the analysis.

## Market-based instruments

### Taxes

A **Pigouvian tax** is set at the marginal external cost, internalising the externality. In
practice the marginal cost is unknown, so the tax is set politically and iterated.

Advantages: revenue, predictable price, simple administration. Disadvantages: the environmental
outcome is uncertain, and the tax is regressive unless revenue is recycled.

### Tradable permits

Cap the total, allocate permits, let the market find the cheapest abatement.

Advantages: the outcome is certain (the cap), cost-effective allocation. Disadvantages: price
volatility, allocation politics, and offsets that may not represent real reductions.

**Taxes fix the price and let quantity float; permits fix the quantity and let price float.**
Choose based on which uncertainty you can tolerate.

### Subsidies and payments for ecosystem services

Pay landholders for providing services — catchment protection, carbon storage, habitat. Works
when the service is measurable and the payment exceeds the opportunity cost.

Failure modes: paying for what would have happened anyway (additionality), and payment ending
when the programme ends (permanence).

### Deposit-refund

Charge at purchase, refund at return. Elegant because it aligns the incentive with the waste
stream, and it works — container deposit schemes achieve high return rates.

## Cost-benefit analysis

### Structure

1. Define the counterfactual.
2. Enumerate impacts.
3. Monetise what can be monetised.
4. Discount to present value.
5. Compare, and test sensitivity.

### Sensitivity analysis

If the conclusion flips when a reasonable input changes by 20%, the conclusion is not robust —
it is a restatement of the input. Always report the range.

### What CBA cannot do

- Value irreversibility properly.
- Handle distribution (it aggregates, so losses to some can be hidden by gains to others).
- Price things nobody has preferences about yet.
- Substitute for a decision about rights.

CBA is a tool for ranking options, not a machine for producing the right answer.

## Limits of the model

Four honest criticisms:

1. **Commensurability** — not everything has a defensible price. Some things are protected
   because of what they are, not what they are worth.
2. **Aggregation** — a large benefit to many can justify an enormous loss to few. Whether that is
   acceptable is an ethical question, not an economic one.
3. **Dynamics** — models assume smooth substitution; ecological systems substitute abruptly or
   not at all.
4. **Power** — who sets the prices, and whose preferences are surveyed, is a political question
   disguised as a technical one.

Knowing these limits is what separates an economics-aware argument from an economics-shaped one.

## Why ethics is not decoration

Every environmental decision contains an implicit answer to three questions:

1. **Who counts?** Humans only, sentient animals, all living things, species, ecosystems, the
   abiotic world?
2. **What counts?** Welfare, autonomy, biodiversity, naturalness, beauty, function?
3. **How do we weigh them?** Aggregate, prioritise, or hold some things inviolable?

People disagree about environmental policy mostly because they answer these differently, and
rarely say so. Naming the framework turns an argument into a discussion.

## Anthropocentric frameworks

Human-centred: nature matters because of what it does for people.

### Utilitarian / consequentialist

Maximise total welfare. Policy is judged by outcomes. This is the default framework of
cost-benefit analysis and of most mainstream environmental economics.

Strength: it takes trade-offs seriously and forces quantification.
Weakness: it can justify sacrificing minorities for aggregate gains, and it struggles with
irreversibility.

### Resource conservation

Nature as stock of resources to be used wisely, for present and future people. Pinchot's
position, and the intellectual ancestor of sustainable development.

Strength: politically durable, works with existing institutions.
Weakness: whatever has no use has no claim.

### Liberal / rights-based

People have rights — to a healthy environment, to clean water, to information, to participation.
The Aarhus Convention is an example in treaty form.

Strength: creates enforceable claims and protects individuals against aggregate reasoning.
Weakness: rights conflict, and extending rights beyond humans is contested.

### Contractual and justice-based

Focus on distribution: who bears environmental burdens. Environmental justice work shows that
pollution is not distributed randomly — it follows political weakness.

Strength: makes the equity dimension explicit, which aggregate frameworks conceal.
Weakness: says little about non-human nature for its own sake.

## Biocentric and ecocentric frameworks

### Biocentrism

All living things have moral standing, not just humans. Albert Schweitzer's "reverence for life";
Paul Taylor's version, in which each organism is a teleological centre of life pursuing its own
good.

Problem: how do you weigh a bacterium against a whale? Some criterion of complexity is usually
smuggled in.

### Ecocentrism

The unit of moral concern is the ecosystem, not the individual. Aldo Leopold's land ethic: "A
thing is right when it tends to preserve the integrity, stability, and beauty of the biotic
community."

Strength: it matches how ecology actually works — relationships and processes, not just
specimens.
Weakness: ecosystems have no clear boundaries, and "integrity" resists operational definition.

### Deep ecology

A specific position: nature has intrinsic value independent of human use, and the required
change is not managerial but cultural — a shift in how people understand themselves in relation
to the biosphere.

Its practical content is often criticised as vague, but its diagnostic claim — that reformist
environmentalism treats symptoms — is a genuine challenge to the others.

### Sentientism

Moral standing follows the capacity to suffer. Extends concern to animals with nervous systems,
says nothing about plants, fungi, or ecosystems.

Strength: a clear and defensible criterion.
Weakness: leaves most of the living world outside the boundary.

## Intrinsic value

The claim that something has value in itself, not as a means. Instrumental value is value as a
means.

The distinction does real work. If a forest has only instrumental value, then a better
substitute — a plantation, a plastic — discharges the obligation. If it has intrinsic value, no
substitute discharges it.

Four common arguments for intrinsic value:

1. **Teleological** — living things pursue their own good, so they have a good of their own.
2. **Aesthetic** — some things are valuable for their beauty, independent of use.
3. **Theological** — creation has value granted by its creator.
4. **Relational** — value arises from relationship and care, not from properties.

Each has been criticised, and none has defeated the others. That is the state of the field, and
pretending otherwise is bad philosophy.

## Applying frameworks

### The same problem, four answers

Take a proposal to drain a wetland for agriculture.

- **Utilitarian**: compare agricultural yield against lost ecosystem services and recreation.
  Probably reject if services are high.
- **Rights-based**: does drainage violate anyone's right to a healthy environment? If yes,
  reject regardless of aggregate benefit.
- **Biocentric**: the wetland's organisms have standing; their loss counts directly.
- **Ecocentric**: does drainage preserve the integrity of the biotic community? Almost certainly
  not.

Note that three of the four reject it for different reasons, and one might accept it. The
disagreement is not about facts.

### Arguing across frameworks

Three moves that work:

1. **Name the framework** you are using, and name theirs.
2. **Find the shared premise** — almost everyone accepts that irreversible loss requires stronger
   justification than reversible loss.
3. **Translate** — express your conclusion in their terms as well as yours.

Three moves that fail:

1. Asserting intrinsic value as if it settles the argument.
2. Treating monetisation as if it were neutral.
3. Accusing the other side of bad faith.

### The irreversibility argument

The strongest cross-framework argument in environmental ethics, because it works in all of them:

- Utilitarians care that future options are destroyed.
- Rights theorists care that future people's choices are foreclosed.
- Biocentrists and ecocentrists care that the entities are gone.

Different reasons, same conclusion. Use it when you need to build a coalition rather than win a
debate.

### Where ethics meets the exam

In extended writing, ethics shows up as:

- Justifying why a conservation target was chosen.
- Explaining why two stakeholders disagree when the science is settled.
- Evaluating whether a policy instrument is fair, not just effective.
- Recognising when a question is asking for a value judgement and answering it as one.

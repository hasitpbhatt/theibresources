## Sources of law

### National

- **Statute** — legislation passed by a legislature. Sets the framework.
- **Regulation** — rules made under statute by an agency. Contains the numbers.
- **Case law** — judicial interpretation. Often where the real meaning lives.
- **Customary and indigenous law** — in many jurisdictions, binding on land and water use.

### International

International environmental law has no world police. It works through consent, reciprocity,
reputation, and trade leverage. That shapes what it can and cannot do.

## Principles

These are the load-bearing ideas. Learn them as arguments, not definitions.

### Precautionary principle

Where there is a threat of serious or irreversible damage, lack of full scientific certainty
shall not be used as a reason for postponing cost-effective measures.

The contested part is always "cost-effective" and "serious". Both are judgement calls, which is
why precaution is invoked by both sides of most disputes.

### Polluter pays

The party responsible for pollution bears the cost of managing it. Simple in principle, hard in
practice: identifying the polluter is easy for a pipe and hard for diffuse agricultural runoff.

### Prevention

Act before damage rather than after. Cheaper, and the only option for irreversible harm.

### Sustainable development

Development that meets present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to
meet theirs. The definition is deliberately broad, which is both its political strength and its
analytical weakness.

### Intergenerational and intragenerational equity

Between generations (future people) and within this one (present people). Environmental problems
usually involve both, and policy that addresses one often neglects the other.

### Common but differentiated responsibilities

All states have a role, but not identical obligations. Wealthy states have historically emitted
more and have more capacity. This principle is why climate negotiations are structured around
differentiation, and why it is politically explosive.

## International agreements

### Structure of a treaty

- **Framework** — general commitments, easy to join.
- **Protocol** — specific targets, harder to join.
- **Annexes** — technical lists, updatable without renegotiating.

This layered structure lets ambition ratchet without reopening the whole agreement.

### Compliance mechanisms

- Reporting requirements.
- Peer review.
- Financial and technical assistance.
- Trade measures against non-parties.
- Name-and-shame.

Note what is missing: enforcement. There is no mechanism to compel a sovereign state.

### Why some treaties work

The Montreal Protocol succeeded on four conditions worth generalising:

1. The harm was clear and attributable.
2. Substitutes existed at acceptable cost.
3. A small number of firms produced the chemicals.
4. The costs were concentrated and the benefits were diffuse but large.

Compare with biodiversity agreements, where the harm is diffuse, the actors are millions, and
the substitutes do not exist.

## Enforcement and compliance

### The compliance gap

A law on paper and a law in effect are different objects. The gap opens through:

- **Under-resourcing** — no inspectors, no laboratories.
- **Capture** — the regulator staffed by the regulated.
- **Ambiguity** — standards written to be unenforceable.
- **Jurisdictional mismatch** — the harm crosses a border the regulator does not.
- **Political cycles** — enforcement follows electoral priorities.

### Instruments of enforcement

| Instrument | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Criminal penalty | Strong deterrent | High burden of proof |
| Civil penalty | Lower burden | Priced in as cost of business |
| Injunction | Stops harm immediately | Requires fast courts |
| Permit withdrawal | Existential for operator | Politically costly |
| Citizen suit | Adds capacity | Requires legal standing |
| Market pressure | Fast, cheap | Only works on visible brands |

### The role of standing

Most environmental harm has no individual victim with a direct injury. Rules on **standing** —
who may sue — therefore determine whether enforcement happens at all. Jurisdictions that allow
public interest standing get more enforcement.

## Case patterns

Rather than memorising cases, learn the patterns they illustrate:

1. **Transboundary harm** — one state's emissions injure another's territory. Tests the limits of
   consent-based law.
2. **Diffuse source** — thousands of small contributors, no single defendant. Defeats
   polluter-pays.
3. **Regulatory capture** — the agency defends the industry it regulates.
4. **Permit as licence** — a permit becomes a right to pollute at the permitted level, and
   reducing it becomes expropriation.
5. **Scientific uncertainty as delay** — the demand for more evidence becomes the strategy.
6. **Indigenous title** — rights predating the statute reshape what the statute can do.

Every one of these appears in ESS case studies. Identify which pattern you are looking at before
you start arguing about it.

### Writing about law in an essay

- Name the instrument, not just "the government".
- Separate the legal question from the ethical one.
- Say who enforces, and with what capacity.
- Say what the counterfactual was — what would have happened without it.
- Acknowledge the compliance gap explicitly; it is the most common omission.

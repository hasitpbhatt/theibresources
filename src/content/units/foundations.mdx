## Why start with systems

Almost every environmental question is really a question about a system: what goes in, what
comes out, what feeds back, and how long it takes to respond. Get the diagram right and the
rest of the topic usually follows.

## Systems and models

A **system** is a set of interacting components bounded by a decision about where to draw the
line. That decision is the interesting part. A lake can be a system, or a component of a
catchment system, or a component of a global water cycle — each framing makes different
questions visible.

Three things to keep straight:

- **Storages** hold matter or energy. A storage has a size and a residence time.
- **Flows** move matter or energy between storages. A flow has a rate.
- **Processes** are what convert one thing into another.

A useful discipline: for any diagram, label every arrow with a rate and a unit. If you cannot
state the unit, you probably have not understood the process yet.

### Transfers and transformations

A **transfer** moves something without changing its form — water flowing downhill, a bird
carrying a seed. A **transformation** changes its form — photosynthesis turning light into
chemical energy, nitrogen fixation turning atmospheric nitrogen into ammonia.

Transfers are usually cheap; transformations usually cost energy and are often the
rate-limiting step. When a system stalls, look for the transformation.

## Energy and equilibria

### The first law, restated usefully

Energy is conserved. That sounds like a truism until you use it as a diagnostic: if energy
appears to vanish from your accounting, you have missed a pathway, usually heat.

### The second law, restated usefully

Every transformation degrades energy quality. This is why food chains are short, why
ecosystems need a constant input, and why "recycling" always costs something.

### Equilibria

- **Static equilibrium**: no change at all. Rare in ecology, common in textbook diagrams.
- **Steady-state equilibrium**: storages stay roughly constant while flows continue. A river
  pool, a mature forest, an adult body.
- **Unstable equilibrium**: a small push moves the system away and it does not return.

The important skill is recognising **negative feedback** (damping, stabilising) against
**positive feedback** (amplifying, destabilising), and being able to say which one dominates
at a given moment.

## Sustainability

Sustainability is not a property of a thing; it is a claim about how long a system can keep
doing what it is doing. That makes it testable. Ask three questions:

1. What is being sustained — a stock, a flow, a function, a way of life?
2. Over what timescale?
3. For whom?

Most disagreements about sustainability dissolve once those three are answered, because the
disagreement was never about facts.

### Carrying capacity and its critics

Carrying capacity is the maximum population a system can support indefinitely. In practice it
is not a fixed number: it shifts with technology, trade, and how much degradation a society
will tolerate. Treat it as a moving threshold, not a ceiling.

## Humans and pollution

Pollution is a **flow** of a substance or form of energy into a system faster than the system
can absorb, disperse, or break it down. That definition does two useful things: it makes
pollution a rate problem, and it explains why the same substance can be harmless in one
context and damaging in another.

### The dose–response relationship

Most pollutants show a threshold, a linear region, and a plateau. Knowing which region you are
in changes the policy answer: below the threshold, monitoring; in the linear region,
proportional reduction; at the plateau, you are already committed to damage.

### Point and non-point sources

Point sources are identifiable and regulable. Non-point sources are diffuse, which is why
agricultural runoff is so much harder to control than a factory outfall — and why the
effective interventions are usually land-use and incentive changes rather than end-of-pipe
filters.

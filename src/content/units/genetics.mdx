## DNA structure and replication

### Structure

Two antiparallel strands of nucleotides wound into a right-handed double helix. Sugar-phosphate
backbone on the outside, bases on the inside, paired A–T and G–C by hydrogen bonds (two and three
respectively — which is why GC-rich DNA is more thermally stable).

The helix completes one turn every ten base pairs, with a major and minor groove. Most
DNA-binding proteins read the major groove.

### Semi-conservative replication

1. **Helicase** unwinds and separates the strands.
2. **Single-strand binding proteins** keep them apart.
3. **Primase** lays down an RNA primer.
4. **DNA polymerase** extends 5′→3′, proofreads, and cannot start a strand.
5. **Leading strand** is synthesised continuously; **lagging strand** in Okazaki fragments.
6. **Ligase** joins the fragments.

The 5′→3′ directionality of polymerase is why replication is asymmetric — an elegant consequence
of the chemistry that makes replication possible at all.

**Telomeres** protect chromosome ends; the end-replication problem means the lagging strand
shortens each cycle. Telomerase extends them in germ cells and stem cells, and is reactivated in
most cancers.

## Transcription and translation

### Transcription

RNA polymerase binds a promoter, unwinds DNA, and synthesises a complementary RNA strand. In
eukaryotes the transcript is processed: 5′ cap, poly-A tail, and **splicing** to remove introns.

Alternative splicing lets one gene produce multiple proteins. This is why gene count is a poor
predictor of organismal complexity.

### Translation

- **mRNA** carries the message; **ribosomes** are the machine; **tRNA** brings amino acids.
- **Codons** are triplets; 64 codons, 61 sense, 3 stop.
- The code is **degenerate** (several codons per amino acid), **unambiguous**, and nearly
  **universal** — the last being strong evidence for common ancestry.

Initiation → elongation (peptide bond formation in the ribosome's peptidyl transferase centre,
which is catalytic RNA) → termination at a stop codon.

### The central dogma, with its exceptions

DNA → RNA → protein, stated by Crick. The exceptions matter:

- **Reverse transcription** — RNA → DNA, in retroviruses and retrotransposons.
- **RNA replication** — RNA → RNA, in some viruses.
- **Prions** — protein → protein conformational information transfer, no nucleic acid involved.

The dogma is a summary of the main pathway, not a law of nature.

## Gene expression

### Prokaryotic control

The **operon** model. The *lac* operon: repressor bound in the absence of lactose; lactose binds
the repressor and releases it; transcription proceeds. Positive control by CAP when glucose is low
— so the operon integrates two signals, which is more sophisticated than most textbook
descriptions suggest.

### Eukaryotic control

Multiple layers:

1. **Chromatin remodelling** — methylation generally silences, acetylation generally activates.
2. **Transcription factors** — activators and repressors.
3. **Post-transcriptional** — splicing, RNA editing, mRNA stability.
4. **Translational** — initiation efficiency, microRNAs.
5. **Post-translational** — cleavage, modification, localisation.

**Epigenetics** is heritable change in expression without change in sequence. Methylation
patterns can be transmitted through cell divisions and, in some cases, across generations — which
is why "genetic" and "heritable" are not synonyms.

### Stem cells

Pluripotent (embryonic) versus multipotent (adult). Therapeutic potential comes from the ability
to become multiple cell types; the ethical debate turns on the source. **Induced pluripotent
stem cells** (reprogramming adult cells) largely dissolve the original ethical objection while
creating new ones about consent and commercialisation.

## Inheritance

### Monohybrid crosses

Alleles segregate; the test cross distinguishes homozygous from heterozygous dominant.

### Dihybrid and linkage

Independent assortment applies to genes on different chromosomes. Genes on the same chromosome are
**linked**, and the recombination frequency between them is a measure of distance — the basis of
genetic mapping.

Complete linkage produces distorted ratios. This is the standard explanation for dihybrid ratios
that deviate from 9:3:3:1, and it is worth knowing before assuming statistical error.

### Beyond simple dominance

- **Incomplete dominance** — intermediate phenotype (snapdragon colour).
- **Codominance** — both expressed (AB blood group).
- **Multiple alleles** — ABO has three alleles for one locus.
- **Polygenic** — continuous traits (height, skin colour).
- **Pleiotropy** — one gene, many effects (sickle cell affects red cells, spleen, bones, pain).
- **Epistasis** — one gene masks another.

Almost nothing interesting is a simple Mendelian trait. Mendel chose well.

### Sex linkage

Genes on the X chromosome show a characteristic pattern: males are hemizygous, so recessive
alleles are expressed in males and transmitted through carrier females. Red-green colour blindness
and haemophilia are the standard examples.

### Pedigrees

Read the pattern, not the individual: recessive, dominant, X-linked recessive, mitochondrial. Each
has a diagnostic signature, and the signature is what the question tests.

## Mutation and genetic disease

### Types

- **Substitution** — missense (different amino acid), nonsense (stop codon), silent.
- **Insertion / deletion** — frameshift if not a multiple of three.
- **Duplication, inversion, translocation** — larger scale, often structural.

### Causes

Spontaneous errors during replication, plus mutagens: UV, ionising radiation, reactive chemicals,
some viral infections.

### Examples worth understanding

- **Sickle cell anaemia** — single substitution in β-globin. Heterozygote advantage against
  malaria explains the allele's persistence in endemic regions. The clearest example of selection
  acting on a human population, and of why "harmful allele" is context-dependent.
- **Cystic fibrosis** — recessive, deletion in CFTR, defective chloride transport, thick mucus.
- **Huntington's** — dominant, trinucleotide repeat expansion, anticipation.

### Screening and counselling

Carrier screening, prenatal testing, preimplantation diagnosis. Benefits are real; so are the
ethical questions about information, choice, disability and pressure.

## Biotechnology and ethics

### Techniques

- **PCR** — exponential amplification using thermal cycling and a thermostable polymerase.
- **Gel electrophoresis** — separation by size using a charge field.
- **DNA sequencing** — chain termination (Sanger) historically, now massively parallel
  short-read and long-read methods.
- **CRISPR-Cas9** — guide RNA directs Cas nuclease to a target sequence; the cell repairs the
  break, often imperfectly.

### Applications

Medicine (diagnosis, gene therapy, insulin production), agriculture (crop improvement, disease
resistance), forensic identification, evolutionary research.

### The ethical questions, separated properly

1. **Somatic vs germline** — editing a patient's cells affects them; editing embryos affects
   descendants who cannot consent. This is the sharpest line in the field.
2. **Therapy vs enhancement** — treating disease versus augmenting traits. The boundary is
   contested and where it is drawn determines what is permissible.
3. **Access and equity** — an expensive intervention available to some creates a two-tier
   biology.
4. **Off-target effects** — unintended edits, with consequences that may only appear later.
5. **Ecological release** — gene drives can spread through wild populations and cannot be
   recalled.

### The evaluation habit

For any biotechnology question, ask: what is the intervention, who bears the risk, who receives
the benefit, is it reversible, and who consented. Those five questions produce a better answer
than any list of pros and cons.

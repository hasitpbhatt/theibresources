## Population dynamics

### The demographic transition

The standard four-stage model: high birth and death rates, then falling death rates, then
falling birth rates, then low both. The model describes a *pattern*, not a law — and the
transition is driven mainly by falling child mortality and by women's education and economic
participation, not by coercion.

**Population momentum** explains why population keeps growing after birth rates fall: a large
cohort of young people entering reproductive age produces more births even at replacement
fertility.

### Age structure

A population pyramid tells you the future faster than any total. A broad base means momentum; a
narrow base means decline; a bulge means a cohort that will need schools, then jobs, then
healthcare, in sequence.

## Urban systems

Cities are efficient in per-capita resource terms and inefficient in absolute terms. Density
reduces transport energy and heating demand, but concentrates waste, water demand and air
pollution.

### Urban heat island

Concrete and asphalt absorb and re-radiate heat; reduced vegetation reduces evaporative
cooling; vehicles and buildings add waste heat. The result is a city several degrees warmer
than its surroundings — a public health issue during heatwaves and a cooling-demand feedback.

Mitigation: street trees, green roofs, reflective surfaces, permeable paving, water features.

### Transport and land use

Urban form determines transport emissions more than vehicle technology does. Sprawl makes
driving compulsory; mixed-use density makes it optional. That is a planning decision, made
decades before the emissions show up.

## Food and agriculture

### Types of system

- **Subsistence** — output consumed locally, low external inputs.
- **Commercial** — output sold, high inputs, high yields per unit labour.
- **Intensive** — high input per unit area.
- **Extensive** — low input per unit area, often grazing.

### The yield debate

Intensification spares land but concentrates pollution. Extensification spreads impact but
requires more land. Neither is universally better; the answer depends on what is scarce — land,
water, labour, or pollution capacity.

### Livestock

Ruminants convert grass, which humans cannot digest, into protein — genuinely useful on
marginal land. They also produce methane and consume feed and water. The argument turns almost
entirely on what the animals eat and where they are raised.

### Food waste

Roughly a third of food produced is wasted. Waste carries the full embodied footprint of
production — land, water, energy, fertiliser — with no nutritional benefit. Interventions are
unfashionable but unusually efficient: cold chains, portioning, date labelling reform,
redistribution.

## Energy and resources

### Classifying resources

- **Renewable** — replenished at or above the rate of use.
- **Non-renewable** — formed far slower than use.
- **Potentially renewable** — renewable only under management: groundwater, timber, fish stocks,
  soil.

That third category does most of the work in policy, because it turns "renewable" into a
conditional claim rather than a property.

**Reserves** are the subset of resources that are economically extractable at current prices
with current technology. Reserves grow when prices rise or technology improves — which is why
"fifty years of oil left" has been announced, wrongly, for a century.

### Fossil fuels

Coal is the highest carbon per unit energy and the cheapest; oil is dense and hard to substitute
in aviation and shipping; gas is the cleanest-burning and most exposed to methane leakage during
extraction and transport. That leakage rate decides the argument about gas.

### Nuclear

Very low operational carbon, high energy density, baseload capable, high capacity factor. Against
that: high capital cost, long build times, long-lived waste, low-probability high-consequence
failure, proliferation, and public acceptance.

The honest framing: nuclear is a choice about which risk you prefer, not a choice between risk
and no risk.

### Renewables

Solar photovoltaic costs have fallen by orders of magnitude in two decades, but output is
intermittent and latitude-dependent — capacity factors of 10–25%. Wind runs 25–45% onshore and
higher offshore. Hydro is dispatchable but geography-limited, and large dams have substantial
social and ecological costs that are usually underweighted. Biomass is carbon-neutral **only**
if regrowth matches harvest, a strong condition frequently violated. Geothermal is constant but
geographically restricted.

### Storage and grids

The central engineering problem of the transition is not generation but **time mismatch**.
Solar produces at midday; demand peaks in the evening.

- **Pumped hydro** — mature, efficient, geography-limited.
- **Lithium batteries** — fast, costly, hours of duration.
- **Flow batteries** — long duration, low energy density.
- **Green hydrogen** — long duration, very low round-trip efficiency.
- **Demand response** — shifting load instead of storing energy. Cheap and underused.
- **Interconnection** — geographic diversity smooths output.

Why the transition is slow: existing plants are already paid for, grids were built for
centralised generation, permitting takes years, storage is not yet cheap at scale, subsidies
persist, and visible change is politically expensive.

## Policy instruments

Four families, each with a characteristic failure mode:

| Instrument | Mechanism | Typical failure |
|---|---|---|
| Regulation | Legal limit | Inflexible; costly compliance; can be captured |
| Market-based (tax, permit) | Price signal | Regressive unless revenue recycled; hard to set |
| Subsidy | Reward for behaviour | Distorts markets; persists past its purpose |
| Information | Persuasion | Weak alone; easily ignored |

### Cap and trade

A cap sets total emissions; permits allocate the right to emit; trading finds the cheapest
abatement. Works when measurement is reliable and the cap genuinely binds. Fails when permits
are over-allocated or offsets are unverifiable.

### Extended producer responsibility

Makes producers responsible for end-of-life. Effective because it moves the cost to whoever
chooses the design. Weak if collection infrastructure is missing.

### Command and control, defended

Regulation is unfashionable but works well for problems with clear thresholds and few actors.
The Montreal Protocol was essentially command and control.

## Evaluating interventions

A checklist that prevents most bad analysis:

1. **Baseline** — compared with what? A counterfactual, or just the situation before?
2. **Attribution** — did the policy cause the change, or did something else?
3. **Leakage** — did the impact move elsewhere rather than disappear?
4. **Rebound** — did efficiency gains get spent on more consumption?
5. **Distribution** — who gained, who paid?
6. **Permanence** — will the effect survive the end of the programme?
7. **Cost per unit of outcome** — compared with alternatives, not against zero.

Most published claims about successful interventions fail at least two of these. Yours should
not.

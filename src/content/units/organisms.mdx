## Form and function

Physiology is mostly one idea repeated: **maximise exchange surface, minimise diffusion
distance, maintain a steep gradient**. Every system in this unit is a variation on that triad.

- Alveoli: many small, thin walls, ventilated.
- Villi: folded, thin, richly supplied with capillaries.
- Gills: lamellae, countercurrent flow.
- Root hairs: extended surface, thin walls.
- Kidney tubules: long, with selective reabsorption.

When a question asks "how is X adapted for exchange", the answer is almost always which of the
three it maximises and how.

## Gas exchange

### In mammals

- **Alveoli** — ~300 million, total surface around 70 m², walls one cell thick, surrounded by
  capillaries. Surfactant reduces surface tension and prevents collapse.
- **Ventilation** — maintains the concentration gradient. Without it, the gradient collapses and
  diffusion stops regardless of surface area.
- **Circulation** — removes oxygenated blood and brings deoxygenated blood, maintaining the
  gradient on the other side.

Note that both ventilation and circulation are gradient maintenance. Surface area alone is
useless without them.

### In insects

Tracheae and tracheoles deliver air directly to tissues. Spiracles can be closed to limit water
loss. Diffusion is adequate for small body sizes; larger insects use abdominal pumping. This is
why insect size correlates with atmospheric oxygen concentration in the fossil record.

### In fish

Gills with countercurrent exchange: water and blood flow in opposite directions, so a
concentration gradient is maintained along the entire length of the lamella, extracting far more
oxygen than parallel flow could.

### In plants

Stomata on leaves, controlled by guard cells. Opening admits CO₂ and loses water — the central
trade-off of terrestrial plant life. Most water loss is an unavoidable consequence of carbon
gain.

Adaptations to reduce loss: thick cuticle, sunken stomata, rolled leaves, CAM metabolism
(stomata open at night).

## Transport in animals

### Blood

- **Plasma** — water, dissolved substances, proteins.
- **Erythrocytes** — biconcave, no nucleus in mammals, packed with haemoglobin. Biconcave shape
  maximises SA:V for gas exchange.
- **Leukocytes** — defence.
- **Platelets** — clotting.

### Haemoglobin

Tetrameric, four haem groups each binding one O₂. Binding is **cooperative**: the first O₂
binding makes subsequent binding easier, producing a sigmoid dissociation curve.

**Bohr effect** — at high CO₂ / low pH, affinity falls and the curve shifts right, unloading more
oxygen where metabolism is highest. This is how oxygen delivery is matched to demand without any
signalling system.

**Fetal haemoglobin** has higher affinity than adult haemoglobin, allowing transfer across the
placenta. Same trick, different context.

### The heart

Double circulation: pulmonary and systemic circuits in series. This maintains pressure for the
systemic circuit while keeping pulmonary pressure low (protecting delicate lung capillaries).

Cardiac cycle: atrial systole, ventricular systole, diastole. Valves prevent backflow; the SAN
sets rhythm; the AVN delays the impulse so atria finish before ventricles contract.

### Vessels

| Vessel | Wall | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Artery | Thick, elastic, muscular | High pressure transport |
| Arteriole | Muscular | Resistance and distribution |
| Capillary | One cell thick | Exchange |
| Venule | Thin | Collection |
| Vein | Thin, valves | Low pressure return |

## Transport in plants

### Xylem

Dead, hollow, lignified vessels and tracheids. Water and dissolved minerals move upward driven by
**transpiration pull**: evaporation at the leaves creates tension, and cohesion between water
molecules transmits that tension down the column.

This is remarkable: the water is under negative pressure, in a metastable state. A bubble breaks
the column (cavitation), which is why xylem is a fragile solution.

### Phloem

Living sieve tubes with companion cells. Transports sucrose and amino acids from source to sink
by **mass flow**: loading at the source lowers water potential, water enters, pressure rises;
unloading at the sink raises water potential, water leaves, pressure falls. The pressure
difference drives flow.

Source and sink are functional definitions, not anatomical ones — a storage root is a sink in
summer and a source in spring.

## Digestion and absorption

### Enzymes by location

| Location | Enzyme | Substrate |
|---|---|---|
| Mouth | Amylase | Starch |
| Stomach | Pepsin (protease) | Proteins |
| Pancreas | Amylase, lipase, proteases | All three |
| Small intestine | Maltase, sucrase, lactase, peptidases | Disaccharides, peptides |

### Absorption

Villi and microvilli multiply surface area; a single layer of epithelium shortens diffusion
distance; a dense capillary network and a lacteal maintain gradients.

- **Monosaccharides and amino acids** — co-transport with Na⁺ across the epithelium, then
  facilitated diffusion into blood.
- **Lipids** — emulsified by bile salts, digested by lipase, absorbed as micelles, reassembled
  into chylomicrons, enter the lacteal (lymph) rather than blood.

That last detail is why fat absorption bypasses the hepatic portal vein.

## Osmoregulation

### The kidney

Nephron: glomerulus (filtration under pressure), proximal convoluted tubule (bulk reabsorption),
loop of Henle (concentration gradient), distal convoluted tubule (fine tuning), collecting duct
(final water reabsorption under ADH control).

### The loop of Henle

A countercurrent multiplier. The descending limb is permeable to water but not solute; the
ascending limb pumps out solute but is impermeable to water. The result is a concentration
gradient in the medulla that allows the collecting duct to extract water.

Desert mammals have long loops; aquatic mammals have short ones. Loop length predicts urine
concentration, and urine concentration predicts habitat.

### ADH

Osmoreceptors detect plasma concentration; the posterior pituitary releases ADH; ADH increases
collecting duct permeability. More ADH, less urine, more concentrated.

## Defence against disease

### Non-specific

- **Barriers** — skin, mucous membranes, stomach acid, lysozyme in tears.
- **Inflammation** — vasodilation, increased permeability, recruitment of phagocytes.
- **Phagocytosis** — neutrophils and macrophages.
- **Fever** — raises temperature, slowing pathogen replication.

### Specific

- **B cells** — produce antibodies; humoral response.
- **T cells** — helper T cells coordinate; cytotoxic T cells kill infected cells.
- **Memory cells** — the basis of long-term immunity and of vaccination.

### Antibodies

Y-shaped proteins with variable regions specific to an antigen. They neutralise, agglutinate, and
mark pathogens for destruction. The variable region is generated by recombination — which is why
the repertoire can cover antigens never encountered.

### Vaccination logic

Expose the immune system to a harmless form of the antigen, generating memory cells without the
risk of disease. **Herd immunity** extends protection to those who cannot be vaccinated, which is
why vaccination is a collective action problem as well as a personal health decision.

### Antibiotic resistance

Bacteria evolve under selection. Every use of an antibiotic selects for resistance; incomplete
courses and agricultural use accelerate it. Resistance is a textbook example of evolution
observable in real time, and of an externality — the cost of resistance is borne by everyone,
while the benefit of use accrues to the individual.

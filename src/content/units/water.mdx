## The hydrological cycle

Water moves through storages — ocean, ice, groundwater, soil, atmosphere, biota — connected by
flows. The storages are wildly unequal: the ocean holds around 97%, ice around 2%, and
everything humans can easily reach is a fraction of one percent.

That imbalance explains a great deal of water politics. The resource is not scarce globally;
it is scarce locally, seasonally, and in usable form.

### Residence times

Residence time is storage divided by flow rate. Atmospheric water turns over in days;
groundwater in decades to millennia. This is why atmospheric humidity responds to weather
while aquifers respond to decades of policy.

### Groundwater

Aquifers recharge slowly and discharge quickly when pumped. Over-abstraction causes land
subsidence, saltwater intrusion in coastal areas, and permanent loss of storage capacity. The
damage is largely irreversible on human timescales, which makes groundwater a governance
problem more than a hydrological one.

## Freshwater and marine systems

### Rivers

Rivers are **lotic** — flowing — and their character changes downstream: wider, warmer,
slower, more sediment, more nutrients. Discharge (`Q = A × v`, area times velocity) is the
master variable. Floods are not anomalies; they are the river behaving normally at high
discharge.

### Lakes

Lakes are **lentic** — standing — and stratify thermally. In summer, warm water floats on cold,
and the two layers stop mixing. Oxygen in the hypolimnion (bottom layer) then depletes until
autumn turnover. This seasonal cycle drives almost everything about lake chemistry.

### Wetlands

Wetlands are the most productive and most undervalued systems. They store water, buffer floods,
trap sediment, cycle nutrients, and hold carbon. Historically drained as "wasteland"; now
recognised as infrastructure.

### Oceans

Oceans absorb heat and carbon dioxide, which makes them a buffer for the climate system and
also a victim of it. Warming drives stratification; acidification follows dissolved CO₂
directly:

```
CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻
```

More dissolved CO₂ means more hydrogen ions means lower pH. The chemistry is simple; the
consequences for calcifying organisms are not.

## Water quality indicators

Useful indicators, with what each actually tells you:

| Indicator | Tells you | Does not tell you |
|---|---|---|
| Dissolved oxygen | Biological activity and pollution load | Which pollutant |
| BOD | Organic pollution (microbial oxygen demand) | Toxic effects |
| Nitrate / phosphate | Nutrient enrichment risk | Biological response |
| Turbidity | Suspended solids | Chemical composition |
| pH | Acid/base status | Cause of the shift |
| Faecal coliforms | Sewage contamination | Other pathogens |
| Macroinvertebrates | Integrated ecological condition | Specific chemistry |

Biological indicators are powerful because they integrate weeks of conditions. Chemical
samples are powerful because they are specific. Use both.

## Eutrophication

The sequence, in order:

1. Nutrient input (usually nitrate and phosphate) exceeds system capacity.
2. Algal growth accelerates — an algal bloom.
3. Surface algae shade submerged plants, which die back.
4. Algae die; aerobic bacteria decompose them.
5. Respiration by decomposers drives dissolved oxygen down.
6. Hypoxia or anoxia kills fish and invertebrates.
7. Anaerobic decomposition releases hydrogen sulphide and ammonia; water turns turbid and
   foul.
8. A different, often cyanobacteria-dominated, community establishes.

The counterintuitive part: the oxygen crash is caused by **too much life**, not by poison.
Eutrophication is a system overshoot.

### Control

Attacking the nutrient source is the only durable fix. Treating symptoms — aeration,
algicides, dredging — buys time at recurring cost. Riparian buffers, wetland restoration, and
fertiliser timing are the structural interventions.

## Water management

- **Dams**: reliable supply, hydropower, flood control — at the cost of sediment trapping,
  fish migration, downstream erosion, and displacement.
- **Desalination**: unlimited source, high energy cost, brine disposal problem.
- **Rainwater harvesting**: low-tech, decentralised, limited by roof area and storage.
- **Demand management**: metering, pricing, efficiency, leak reduction — usually the cheapest
  water "source" available.

The pattern worth remembering: supply-side solutions are visible and politically attractive;
demand-side solutions are cheap and boring. The boring ones usually win on cost per litre.
